The challenge

Making compliance feel like real preparation.

First Aid training can easily become slide-heavy and passive. I wanted the practical component to feel closer to the conditions learners may actually face: limited time, incomplete information, other people needing direction, equipment to locate and a casualty whose condition may change.

The workshop was therefore built around repeated practical rehearsal rather than long blocks of presentation. Learners worked through realistic maritime and onboard scenarios, made decisions, communicated with each other and then completed related reporting and handover activities.

Design principle

Give learners enough structure to stay safe and competent, but enough uncertainty that they still have to think.

Workshop design

Built around activity, not waiting.

The session structure was designed so learners were continually responding, assisting, observing, communicating, documenting or receiving feedback.

01

Rotating team roles

Primary responder, assistant responder and observer / emergency-services / recorder roles rotate so each learner remains actively involved.

02

Practical stations

CPR and First Aid are divided into focused stations with equipment, scenario prompts, assessor observation and repeated skill rehearsal.

03

Unknown scenarios

Learners can receive an emergency scenario without prior knowledge, requiring them to assess what is happening before deciding what to do.

Observe Decide Respond Communicate
04

Complication cards

Worsening symptoms, rescuer fatigue, interference, noise or delayed response can be introduced to add realistic pressure.

05

Communication & handover

Learners delegate tasks, communicate with responders, escalate care and provide a clear verbal handover.

06

Incident reporting

Practical response flows into documentation, with additional details supplied progressively to simulate workplace information gathering.

Context matters

Maritime scenarios gave the training a reason to feel real.

Scenarios were contextualised to environments learners could plausibly encounter: vessels, marinas, confined spaces, wet decks, delayed emergency response and crew-based teamwork.

Practical examples

What learners worked through.

Scenarios were selected to cover required practical skills while still feeling like operational situations rather than isolated demonstrations.

CPR

Adult collapse + AED

DRSABCD, CPR, AED use, rescuer changeover, communication and handover.

CPR

Infant CPR

Response and breathing checks, infant CPR technique, reassessment and handover.

First Aid

Asthma + anaphylaxis

Condition recognition, medication assistance, airway monitoring and escalation.

First Aid

Bleeding + shock

PPE, wound management, monitoring, reassurance, escalation and reporting.

First Aid

Fractures + immobilisation

Injury assessment, splinting or sling use, circulation checks and casualty support.

First Aid

Choking + envenomation

Rapid response, pressure immobilisation, choking management and escalation.

Assessment approach

Practice can be coached. Competency still has to be demonstrated.

During formative practice, learners can receive prompting, repetition and feedback. Final competency decisions are based on the learner's own observed performance and the evidence required for the relevant unit.

The workshop also supports mixed experience levels. More experienced learners can take on additional complications or team-lead responsibilities, while less experienced learners can build confidence through guided practice before final assessment.
